# Ace Operations, People & Customer Service Knowledge Base
## Running the Day-to-Day Well

This volume covers documenting and improving processes, managing projects and meetings, the
employee lifecycle from job description to exit, and handling customers when something goes

# SECTION 65: PROCESS AND SOPs

## Writing a Standard Operating Procedure

An SOP exists so the work happens the same way without the person who invented it. It is not
documentation for its own sake; if nobody follows it, it has failed.

**A usable SOP is short and has five parts:** what triggers it, who owns it, the numbered steps,
what "done" looks like, and who to escalate to when it goes wrong. A checklist someone actually
follows beats a manual nobody opens.

**Write it as the person doing it, not about them.** "Open the booking system and filter to
today" — concrete, in order, with the exact names of the buttons and screens.

**Have the person who does the work write it,** while doing it, the first time you delegate.
Knowledge then lives in the business rather than in one head, and the next hire trains
themselves from it.

**Include the judgment calls, not just the clicks.** The steps are the easy part; what makes an
SOP valuable is "if the customer has already been contacted twice, do X instead".

**Date it and name an owner.** An SOP nobody owns drifts out of date, and a wrong SOP is worse
than none because people follow it.

## What to Document First

Frequency times cost-of-error. Document the things done often, the things that are expensive
when wrong, and the things only you can currently do — in that order. Everything else can wait.

**Every recurring mistake deserves a process change, not a reprimand.** If someone makes the
same error twice, the process permits it. Add the check to the process instead of asking people
to be more careful, which never scales.

## Improving a Process

**Map it as it actually is,** not as it is supposed to be. Walk it end to end and write down each
step and handoff. The gap between the two is usually where the problem lives.

**Find the constraint.** Throughput is set by the single tightest step; improving anything else
just moves work into a queue in front of the real bottleneck.

**Remove before you automate.** Automating a wasteful process makes waste faster and harder to
change. Ask what steps exist only because of history, then simplify, then document, then
automate.

**Count the handoffs.** Each one is a delay and a chance to drop something. Fewer people touching
a piece of work is usually faster and more reliable than more.

# SECTION 66: PROJECTS AND MEETINGS

## Managing a Project

**Define done before starting.** A project without a written definition of completion expands
indefinitely and is impossible to judge.

**Name one owner.** Shared ownership of an outcome reliably produces no ownership. Contributors
can be many; the person accountable is one.

**Break it into pieces that fit in a week.** Anything longer cannot be tracked meaningfully —
"in progress" for three weeks tells you nothing about whether it is on course.

**Track what is blocked, not just what is being worked on.** Blocked items are where projects
quietly die, and they need a person and a date, not a status.

**Scope, time and resources trade against each other.** When something slips, one of the three
has to give — decide which deliberately rather than hoping. Cutting scope is usually the least
damaging and the most resisted.

**Review at the end honestly.** What took longer than expected, what was missed at the start,
what to do differently. Written down, this is how estimating improves; unwritten, the same
mistakes recur indefinitely.

## Running Meetings Worth Attending

**No agenda, no meeting.** The agenda states the decisions to be made, not the topics to be
discussed. If there is no decision, it is probably a written update.

**Invite only people who decide or do.** Attendance for information is a message.

**Default to shorter.** Work expands to fill the slot; a 25-minute meeting frequently achieves
what an hour would.

**End with who does what by when,** written down and sent. A meeting without recorded actions
usually produces none.

**The recurring meeting audit:** any standing meeting should have to justify continuing. Most
weekly meetings outlive their purpose by months.

**Prefer written for status, meetings for decisions and disagreement.** Written updates respect
everyone's time and produce a record; live discussion is for the things that genuinely need
back-and-forth.

# SECTION 67: THE EMPLOYEE LIFECYCLE

## Writing a Job Description

**Lead with outcomes, not duties.** What will this person have achieved in twelve months, stated
measurably. If you cannot answer that, you are not ready to hire and will end up judging
candidates on likeability.

**Include:** the outcomes, the day-to-day reality, what success looks like at 90 days, who they
work with, the genuinely required skills separated from the nice-to-have, the pay range, and the
location or remote arrangement.

**Publish the range.** Withholding it wastes everyone's time and filters out good candidates who
will not gamble on a process.

**Cut the requirements list.** Long lists of "must haves" deter qualified applicants,
disproportionately those who assess themselves conservatively, and rarely reflect what the job
actually needs.

## Onboarding

The first weeks determine whether someone becomes productive or quietly disengages, and most
small businesses do nothing deliberate here.

**Before day one:** equipment, accounts, access, and a first-week plan ready. A new starter
waiting two days for a login learns what the organisation is like.

**Give them something real to finish in week one.** A small completed piece of work builds more
confidence than a fortnight of reading.

**Assign someone to answer questions** so they are not choosing between guessing and interrupting
their manager.

**Set explicit expectations for 30, 60 and 90 days,** and review against them. Most
disappointing hires are cases where nobody said clearly what good looked like.

**Ask for their observations at the end of the first month.** They can see what is confusing in a
way nobody else can, and they never will again.

## Performance and Feedback

**Feedback is immediate, specific and private for correction; immediate, specific and public for
praise.** Feedback saved for a review months later is useless, because the behaviour has been
reinforced meanwhile.

**Separate coaching from evaluation.** Ongoing conversation about how the work is going is a
different thing from a formal assessment tied to pay, and conflating them makes people defensive
in exactly the conversations that should be candid.

**A useful review covers:** what was achieved against expectations, what to develop, what support
is needed, and what the next period's expectations are. No surprises — anything raised in a
review should have been raised when it happened.

**Address a problem early and directly.** Most performance issues that end badly were visible
months before anyone said anything, and by then the pattern is established and the person
reasonably feels ambushed.

## Ending Employment

**Do the documentation first.** Specific, dated records of the problem, what was communicated,
what support was offered, and what happened next. This is the difference between a defensible
decision and an expensive one.

**Have the conversation in person, briefly and clearly.** State the decision, do not debate it,
cover the practicalities, and treat the person with dignity. It should not be a surprise if
feedback has been honest.

**Get advice before terminating anyone who has recently complained, taken protected leave, or
raised a safety or legal concern** — the timing creates an appearance of retaliation regardless
of intent.

**Handle the practicalities properly:** final pay according to your state's rules, accrued leave,
benefits continuation information, return of equipment, and access revoked promptly.

**Remember the people who stay.** How a departure is handled tells the rest of the team what the
organisation is. Mishandled exits damage morale more than the departure itself.

# SECTION 68: CUSTOMER SERVICE

## Handling an Angry Customer

**Let them finish.** Interrupting to correct a detail escalates reliably. Most of the heat
dissipates once someone feels heard.

**Acknowledge the impact before explaining the cause.** "That meant you missed your deadline —
I'm sorry" lands; "our supplier let us down" sounds like an excuse, however true it is.

**Apologise for the experience without necessarily admitting fault.** You can be genuinely sorry
something happened without conceding liability.

**Move to the fix quickly and be specific.** "I'll have this replaced and with you Thursday, and
I'll email you the tracking today" ends a complaint. "We'll look into it" prolongs it.

**Give them a choice where you can.** Refund, replacement, or credit returns some control to
someone who feels they have none.

**Take it out of public.** Respond to a public complaint briefly and without defensiveness, then
move the detail to a private channel. Arguing publicly damages you more than the original
complaint did.

**Follow up after it is resolved.** A short check that everything is now right converts a fair
number of complainants into unusually loyal customers.

## Service Recovery

Recovering a failure well often produces more loyalty than never failing — customers who have
seen you handle a problem know what happens when things go wrong, which is exactly the
uncertainty that stops people committing.

**Speed matters more than the size of the gesture.** A fast small remedy beats a generous slow
one.

**Fix the cause, not just the instance.** A complaint is a free report from your process. If two
customers report the same thing, it is a process problem, and compensating each one individually
is paying repeatedly to avoid a fix.

## Reducing Support Volume

**Categorise contacts for a month.** The top three reasons are usually a large majority, and they
are usually preventable rather than inherent.

**The common causes:** unclear expectations set before purchase, missing information on the
website, an onboarding step people get stuck at, and proactive updates not sent — "where is my
order" is almost entirely a communication failure rather than a delivery one.

**Answer publicly what you answer repeatedly.** A good FAQ, product page detail, or setup guide
removes the contact permanently.

**Proactive beats reactive.** Telling someone about a delay before they ask converts a complaint
into a reassurance.

**Do not optimise for deflection alone.** Making it hard to reach a human reduces tickets and
increases churn, which is the wrong trade.

## Refunds and Policy

**Write the policy plainly and make it easy to find.** Ambiguity produces disputes and
chargebacks, which cost more than the refund would have.

**Cover:** the window, the condition, who pays return costs, whether it is refund or credit, and
how long processing takes.

**Honour it without friction when it applies.** A grudging refund buys a bad review; a graceful
one sometimes buys a repeat customer.

**Track refund reasons** for the same reason you track return reasons — they identify a product,
expectation, or process problem you can fix at source.
