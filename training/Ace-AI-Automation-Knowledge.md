# Ace AI & Automation Knowledge Base
## Using AI Sensibly in a Small Business

This volume covers where AI genuinely helps a small business, where it does not, how to automate
work without making a mess, and how to think about the risks honestly.

**A note on honesty when discussing this.** AI is heavily oversold, and business owners are
being sold it constantly. The useful position is neither hype nor dismissal: be specific about
what works today, plain about what does not, and clear that a tool which produces confident
wrong answers needs a human check wherever the cost of being wrong is real.

# SECTION 69: WHERE AI ACTUALLY HELPS

## Should I Use ChatGPT for My Business?

Yes, for the right work — and the same answer applies to the other general assistants such as
Claude, Gemini and Copilot, which differ in detail rather than in kind. They are genuinely
useful for drafting, summarising, rewriting, classifying and explaining, and genuinely unreliable
for specific facts, figures, dates and arithmetic. The sections below set out which is which.

**Practical starting advice:** use a business or team tier rather than a personal one, because
the terms on data retention and training usually differ. Decide what may and may not be pasted
in before people start using it. Begin with one repetitive writing task rather than trying to
apply it everywhere at once. And check anything factual before it leaves the business.

## The Work It Is Genuinely Good At

**Drafting.** First drafts of emails, listings, descriptions, posts, proposals and outlines.
Starting from something and editing is faster than starting from nothing, and editing is where
the human value is.

**Summarising and extracting.** Long documents, meeting notes, review threads, transcripts.
Pulling structured information out of unstructured text is one of the most reliable applications.

**Rewriting and adapting.** The same message shortened, made more formal, aimed at a different
audience, or turned into five variations for testing.

**Answering questions about your own material** when it has been given the material to read.
This is different from asking it to recall facts, and much more reliable.

**Classifying and routing.** Sorting enquiries by topic, tagging feedback by theme, flagging
which messages need a human first.

**Writing and explaining code,** particularly boilerplate, small utilities, and explaining an
unfamiliar error message.

## Where It Falls Down

**Specific facts, figures, dates and citations.** A language model will produce a confident,
plausible, wrong number, form name, statute, or reference. Anything factual and consequential
needs verification against a source.

**Recent events,** unless the tool is genuinely retrieving current information rather than
recalling training data.

**Arithmetic and reconciliation.** Do not trust it to total an invoice or reconcile accounts.
Have it structure the work and let software do the maths.

**Judgment with real consequences** — legal, tax, medical, hiring, or safety decisions. It can
help you prepare for the conversation; it is not a substitute for the professional.

**Anything requiring accountability.** A model cannot be responsible for an outcome. Where
someone must be answerable, a human reviews before it goes out.

**Your specific context, unless you supply it.** It does not know your customers, your pricing,
or your constraints unless you tell it, and it will fill gaps with generic plausibility.

## Getting Better Results

**Give context, not just an instruction.** Who it is for, what you are trying to achieve, the
constraints, and an example of what good looks like. The difference between a vague prompt and a
well-specified one is larger than the difference between most tools.

**Ask for a specific format** — a table, five options, under 100 words, in this structure. It
removes most of the editing.

**Iterate rather than restart.** Say what is wrong with the draft and ask for a revision; that is
usually faster than rewriting the prompt from scratch.

**Give it your material** rather than relying on its memory. Paste the document, the notes, the
data. Grounded answers are dramatically more accurate than recalled ones.

**Ask it to say when it does not know.** And treat any specific fact as unverified until checked.

# SECTION 70: AUTOMATING WORK

## What to Automate First

**Frequency times time saved, minus the cost of it going wrong.** Automate the thing done daily
that takes ten minutes before the thing done annually that takes a day.

**Good first candidates:** appointment reminders and confirmations, invoice generation and
payment chasing, review requests after a job, lead capture into a pipeline, recurring reports,
data moving between two systems someone is currently retyping, and file organisation.

**Poor candidates:** anything where an error is expensive and unnoticed, anything requiring
judgment about a specific customer, anything that changes constantly, and anything you do rarely.

**Simplify before automating.** Automating a process with unnecessary steps encodes the waste and
makes it harder to remove. Map it, cut it, then automate what remains.

**Automate the boring middle, keep the human at the ends.** A person decides what to do and
checks what went out; the machine does the repetitive part between.

## Building It Without Making a Mess

**Start with one process, end to end.** A single automation that reliably works is worth more
than five half-built ones, each of which someone has to remember the state of.

**Handle the failure case explicitly.** What happens when the data is missing, the service is
down, or the format changes? An automation that fails silently is worse than a manual process,
because nobody notices until the damage accumulates.

**Log what it did** so you can answer "did that go out?" without guessing.

**Keep a manual fallback** for anything customer-facing. Systems break at inconvenient times.

**Review automations periodically.** They keep running long after the reason for them has
changed, quietly doing the wrong thing.

**Watch the dependency.** Automation built on a specific tool becomes a switching cost. Prefer
approaches where your data remains yours and exportable.

## Practical Guidance on Cost and Tools

Categories worth knowing rather than specific products, which change: scheduling and reminders,
form and lead capture, CRM and pipeline, email sequences, document generation, bookkeeping
integration, and general connector tools that join two systems together without code.

**Count the total cost.** Per-seat pricing across six tools adds up faster than owners expect, and
several tools with overlapping functions is a common and avoidable expense. Audit the stack
yearly.

**A connector tool is often cheaper than custom code** for joining two systems, and far cheaper to
maintain. Reserve custom development for the thing that is actually your business.

# SECTION 71: RISKS AND STAFF

## Confidentiality

**Assume anything pasted into a third-party tool may be retained** unless the terms say
otherwise. Customer personal data, contracts, financials, and anything under an NDA deserve a
deliberate decision rather than a habit.

**Check the terms on training.** Business tiers commonly exclude your content from training where
consumer tiers do not. This is worth reading rather than assuming.

**Locally-run models keep data on your own hardware,** which removes the question entirely for
sensitive material. The trade is capability and speed against control.

**Write a short internal policy** — what may and may not be pasted into which tools. Most
accidental disclosure is well-intentioned people not having been told.

## Accuracy and Liability

**You are responsible for what your business publishes and sends,** whatever produced it. A
wrong price, an invented statistic, or a bad promise in an AI-drafted email is your problem, not
the tool's.

**Review anything that leaves the business,** anything that touches money, and anything that
makes a commitment to a customer.

**Do not present generated content as professional advice.** Drafting a response about a tax or
legal question is not the same as answering it.

## Staff and Jobs

Owners and employees both ask this, and it deserves a straight answer rather than reassurance.

**What is actually happening is task-level, not job-level.** AI is good at pieces of jobs —
drafting, summarising, first-pass sorting — and poor at the accountability, relationships,
judgment and physical work that most roles are largely made of. Roles change shape faster than
they disappear.

**The most exposed work is high-volume routine text handling.** The least exposed is anything
involving physical presence, trust, negotiation, accountability, or genuinely novel judgment.

**For a small business the realistic gain is capacity, not headcount.** It lets a small team
produce more and respond faster, which usually means growing without hiring proportionally
rather than replacing anyone.

**Involve staff rather than surprising them.** People who are told a tool will make their day
easier and are asked where it would help adopt it; people who suspect it is there to replace them
quietly resist it, and the implementation fails.

**Be honest about the limits when you introduce it.** The fastest way to lose a team's trust in a
tool is to oversell it and have them find the errors themselves.
