## Protecting the Business, the Work, and the Relationships

**Scope and limits — read this first.** This describes general United States practice for small
businesses. Laws differ by state and country, they change, and nothing here is legal advice. Use
it to understand what a thing is, when it matters, and what question to take to a lawyer — then
confirm specifics with a qualified attorney before acting. Never invent a statute, a filing fee,
a deadline, or a form number; if a specific is not known with confidence, say so and point to
the authoritative source.

Anything involving a dispute, a signature on something material, an employee exit, or real money
is worth an hour of an attorney's time. That hour is almost always cheaper than the alternative.

# SECTION 59: INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY

## The Four Kinds, and Which You Actually Need

**Copyright** protects original creative expression — writing, photographs, music, video, code,
designs. In the US it exists automatically the moment the work is fixed in tangible form; you do
not have to register it to own it. Registration is what unlocks the stronger remedies, including
statutory damages and attorney's fees, and is generally required before you can sue. For a small
business the practical position is: you already own your website copy, your photos and your
code, and registering matters when the work is commercially significant enough that you would
actually litigate over it.

**Trademark** protects the identifiers customers use to recognise you — a business name, a logo,
a slogan. In the US, rights arise from actually using the mark in commerce, but federal
registration gives nationwide protection and much stronger enforcement. Register when the brand
is becoming an asset, when you are expanding beyond one area, or when you need to stop someone
using a confusingly similar name.

**Patent** protects an invention — how something novel works. Expensive, slow, and technical,
and for most small businesses not the right tool. A provisional application is a cheaper way to
establish an early date and buy a year to decide, but it must be followed by a full application
or it lapses. If your advantage is speed, service, or relationships rather than a novel
mechanism, patents are not where to spend money.

**Trade secret** protects valuable information *because* it is secret — a customer list, a
process, a formula, pricing models. It costs nothing to obtain and lasts as long as secrecy
does, but it is only protected if you actually take reasonable steps to keep it secret:
confidentiality agreements, access controls, and marking things confidential. Disclose it
publicly and it is gone permanently.

## "How Do I Protect My Idea?"

**An idea by itself generally cannot be protected.** Copyright covers the expression of an idea,
not the idea; patents cover an invention that is novel and non-obvious and require disclosure
in exchange. There is no mechanism that gives you ownership of a concept because you thought of
it first.

**What actually protects a business is execution and position** — the relationships, the brand,
the accumulated data, the operational learning, the distribution. This is not a consolation
prize; it is the honest answer, and it is why competitors who copy the concept usually do not
take the business.

**What to do in practice:** file a trademark on the name once it matters, register copyright on
significant creative work, use confidentiality agreements where you must disclose something
genuinely valuable, keep the actually-secret things actually secret, and move faster than the
people who might copy you.

## Ownership of Work

**A contractor generally owns what they create unless the contract says otherwise.** This
surprises people badly and regularly. The developer who built your site, the designer who drew
your logo, the photographer who shot your premises — absent written assignment, they may retain
copyright and you may hold only an implied licence to use it.

**Every contractor agreement should assign intellectual property to you in writing,** signed
before the work starts. Retrofitting an assignment after a relationship sours is expensive and
sometimes impossible.

**Employees are treated differently** — work created within the scope of employment usually
belongs to the employer — but an explicit written term still avoids argument, particularly at
the boundaries.

**Check what you actually own before you sell a business.** Unassigned logos and code are found
during diligence, and they reduce the price or delay the deal.

## NDAs — When They Help and When They Do Not

A non-disclosure agreement obliges the recipient to keep information confidential and not use it
for their own benefit. **One-way** protects a single discloser; **mutual** protects both, and is
normal when two businesses are exploring working together.

**When an NDA is genuinely warranted:** disclosing a process, financials, customer data, source
code, or unreleased plans to a vendor, contractor, potential partner, or acquirer.

**When it is not:** pitching to most investors, who generally decline to sign them and see the
request as inexperience; casual conversations; or anything you would say publicly anyway. An NDA
also does nothing for information the recipient already knew or can obtain independently.

**Understand what it costs to enforce.** An NDA is a contract; enforcing it means proving breach
and loss, and litigating. Its practical value is often deterrent and clarity rather than remedy —
which is a reason to use it where it fits and not to treat it as protection for an idea.

**Terms worth checking:** exactly what counts as confidential, how long the obligation lasts,
the standard exclusions (already known, publicly available, independently developed, required by
law), and what happens to materials at the end.

# SECTION 60: THE DOCUMENTS A BUSINESS NEEDS

## Website Terms and Privacy Policy

**A privacy policy is required in practice** — by privacy laws where they apply to you, by app
stores, by payment processors, and by advertising platforms. It should honestly state what data
you collect, why, who you share it with, how long you keep it, and how someone can access or
delete it.

**Write it to match what you actually do.** A copied policy describing data practices you do not
have is worse than none: it is a public statement that is untrue.

**Terms of service** set the rules — acceptable use, payment and refunds, limitation of
liability, termination, and which jurisdiction's law applies. Anything with accounts,
subscriptions, or user-generated content needs them.

**Cookie consent** is required in some jurisdictions, particularly for non-essential and
advertising cookies. Consent must be genuine, which is why pre-ticked boxes do not satisfy it.

**Privacy law reaches further than people expect.** European rules can apply to a business
outside Europe that handles the data of people in Europe; several US states have their own
regimes with their own thresholds. If you are collecting personal data at any scale, that is a
conversation to have with an attorney rather than a template to copy.

## Contracts That Matter Most

**A service agreement** — scope and exclusions, price and payment schedule, timeline, ownership
of work, termination and notice, liability limits, dispute resolution.

**An independent contractor agreement** — the above, plus IP assignment and language reflecting
a genuine contractor relationship rather than employment.

**A partnership or operating agreement** — the most important document a multi-owner business
signs, and the one most often skipped. Ownership split, decision rights and deadlock breaking,
capital contributions, profit distribution, roles, and a buy-sell clause covering death,
disability, divorce, and exit. Negotiate it while everyone is friendly.

**A lease** — the largest fixed commitment most small businesses make. Check assignability, what
happens at renewal, who pays for what maintenance, personal guarantee terms, and whether the
permitted use covers what you actually intend to do there.

**Practical contract habits:** get it in writing including with friends; read the termination and
liability clauses first, because that is where the risk lives; never sign something you have not
read because you were told it is standard; and keep signed copies somewhere you can find them.

## Employment Basics

**Most US employment is at-will,** meaning either side can end it at any time for any lawful
reason — but the exceptions matter enormously. You cannot terminate for a protected
characteristic, for whistleblowing, for taking legally protected leave, or in retaliation for a
complaint.

**Document performance problems as they happen,** with dates and specifics. A file showing a
pattern and a chance to improve is the difference between a defensible termination and an
expensive one.

**Get advice before firing anyone who has recently complained, taken leave, or raised a safety or
legal concern.** Timing creates the appearance of retaliation even where none exists.

**Final pay rules vary by state** — some require payment immediately on termination. So do rules
on accrued leave payout, final expenses, and notice.

**Classification, wage and hour, and overtime rules are where small businesses get caught.**
Misclassifying an employee as a contractor, or an hourly worker as exempt from overtime, creates
back-pay liability with penalties.

**Anything with a handbook, an exit, or a complaint in it is worth an attorney's time.**

# SECTION 61: RISK, INSURANCE AND DISPUTES

## Business Insurance

**General liability** — third-party injury and property damage. Frequently required by
landlords and commercial clients before they will sign.

**Professional liability (errors and omissions)** — claims that your advice or service caused a
loss. Essential for consultants, agencies, and anyone giving professional advice.

**Property** — your premises, equipment and inventory.

**Workers' compensation** — generally mandatory once you have employees, with thresholds varying
by state.

**Cyber liability** — breach response, notification costs, and related liability. Increasingly
relevant to anyone holding customer data.

**Insure the catastrophic, self-insure the routine.** Premiums on small predictable losses are
convenience bought at a markup; the point of insurance is the loss that would end the business.

**Read the exclusions,** which is where the actual coverage is defined, and tell your broker
honestly what the business does — a policy issued on an inaccurate description may not respond
when you need it.

## The Liability Shield

Forming an LLC or corporation separates business liabilities from personal assets, but the
protection is conditional on behaving like a separate entity.

**What undermines it:** mixing personal and business finances, failing to keep required records
or filings, undercapitalising the business, and signing personally without realising it.

**Personal guarantees pierce the shield voluntarily.** Landlords, lenders, and some suppliers
routinely ask for one, and signing moves the risk to your household regardless of the entity.
Know when you are being asked, and negotiate the scope and duration if you can.

## Handling a Dispute

**Respond, do not go quiet.** Silence turns negotiable problems into legal ones, and in the case
of a formal complaint or lawsuit, missing a deadline can lose the matter by default.

**Preserve everything the moment a dispute is foreseeable** — emails, messages, files, records.
Deleting relevant material, even routinely, can be treated as spoliation and is far more damaging
than whatever the material said.

**Do not argue in public,** including in review responses and on social media. It creates a
permanent record, and observers remember the fight rather than the merits.

**Most disputes should settle.** Litigation is slow, expensive, public, and consumes attention
that the business needs. A mediocre settlement now regularly beats a favourable judgment years
away.

**Small claims court** exists for genuinely small amounts, is designed to be used without a
lawyer, and is often the proportionate route for an unpaid invoice.

**When you receive anything formal** — a demand letter, a complaint, a regulatory notice — get an
attorney before responding. An informal reply intended to be helpful can concede facts you would
rather have contested.
