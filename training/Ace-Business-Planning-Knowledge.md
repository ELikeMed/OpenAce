# Ace Business Planning & Market Entry Knowledge Base
## Building a Plan, Sizing a Market, and Deciding Whether to Start

This volume covers business plans, go-to-market strategy, market research, competitor
analysis, feasibility studies, and validating an idea before committing money. It is
industry-agnostic. Figures are conventional rules of thumb that vary by market.

# SECTION 25: WRITING A BUSINESS PLAN

## What a Business Plan Is Actually For

A business plan has two possible purposes and they produce different documents. An
**internal plan** is a thinking tool: it forces you to make your assumptions explicit so you
can test the risky ones. An **external plan** is a persuasion document for a lender,
investor, or landlord. Most people write the external one when they need the internal one.

The internal plan is the more valuable of the two. Its job is to surface the two or three
assumptions the whole business rests on — that customers will pay X, that you can acquire
them for under Y, that you can deliver at Z cost — so you can go test them cheaply before
committing.

**A plan is not a prediction; it is a set of testable claims.** Judge it by whether it makes
your assumptions falsifiable, not by how confident it sounds.

## The Structure of a Business Plan

**Executive summary.** Written last, read first. One page: what the business does, who for,
why it wins, what it needs, what the returns look like.

**Problem and customer.** Who specifically has this problem, how they solve it today, and
what that current solution costs them in money, time, or risk. Be specific enough to name
actual companies or describe an actual person.

**Solution and offer.** What you sell, what it costs, what the customer gets. Not features —
the outcome and the price.

**Market.** Size, growth, and structure. How many potential customers exist, how much they
spend, who else serves them.

**Competition.** Who else solves this, including doing nothing. Why a customer chooses you.

**Go-to-market.** How you reach customers, in what order, at what cost per customer.

**Operations.** How the thing actually gets delivered, by whom, at what capacity.

**Team.** Who does the work and what evidence exists that they can.

**Financials.** Startup costs, unit economics, a monthly cash forecast for 12–24 months, and
break-even. This is the section lenders read most carefully.

**Risks and assumptions.** The section most people omit and the one that signals seriousness.
List what has to be true, and what you will do if it isn't.

## The One-Page Plan

For most small businesses, a one-page plan beats a forty-page document because it gets
revised. Fill in these sentences and you have a working plan:

- We help **[specific customer]** who struggle with **[specific problem]**
- by **[what you do]**, which produces **[measurable outcome]**.
- We reach them through **[primary channel]** at roughly **[cost per customer]**.
- They pay **[price/model]**, costing us **[delivery cost]**, leaving **[contribution margin]**.
- We win because **[advantage a competitor cannot easily copy]**.
- This works only if **[key assumption]** is true, which we will test by **[cheap test]**.

If you cannot complete those, the gaps are exactly where the work is.

## Common Business Plan Mistakes

**A market size that proves nothing.** "The industry is $50 billion" tells a reader nothing
about whether you can get customers. Size the market you can actually reach.

**Revenue projections built backwards from a goal.** Hockey-stick charts with no mechanism
underneath. Build revenue up from units: customers × frequency × price, with each number
traceable to something observable.

**No competitors listed.** Claiming no competition reads as not having looked. Every business
competes with the status quo at minimum.

**Ignoring cash timing.** Profitable-on-paper plans that run out of cash in month seven are
the most common failure in an otherwise sound plan.

# SECTION 26: MARKET RESEARCH AND SIZING

## Sizing a Market Honestly

Three layers, from broad to real:

**Total market** — everyone who could conceivably buy this category. Useful only for context.
**Serviceable market** — those you could actually reach and serve, given your geography,
capacity, language, and channel.
**Obtainable market** — the share you can realistically win in the next few years given your
resources and competition. This is the number that matters.

**Build it bottom-up, not top-down.** Top-down: "the market is $2B, we'll get 1%." That 1% is
invented. Bottom-up: "there are 340 dental practices within 30 miles, roughly 15% would
consider switching in a given year, at $9,000 average contract." Now every number is
inspectable and arguable, which is the point.

**Sanity-check against capacity.** A market you cannot serve is not your market. If you can
deliver to 40 customers a year, a 10,000-customer market and a 400-customer market lead to
the same plan.

## Desk Research — What to Look At

**Industry associations and trade publications** for structure, margins, and norms.
**Government data** — census and labour statistics for population, business counts, and
spending patterns by area. **Public company filings** for a category's real economics, since
public competitors must disclose what private ones won't.

**Search behaviour tells you about demand.** Keyword volume for what customers search shows
whether people are actively looking for a solution or don't know they have the problem —
which completely changes the go-to-market.

**Review sites are free customer research.** Read one- and two-star reviews of your
competitors. They tell you precisely what the market is dissatisfied with, in customers' own
words, which becomes your positioning and your ad copy.

## Talking to Customers — The Research That Matters Most

Fifteen conversations with potential customers is worth more than any report. The failure
mode is asking people to predict their future behaviour, which they cannot do.

**Ask about the past, not the future.** Not "would you buy this?" — everyone is polite. Ask:
when did you last have this problem, what did you do about it, what did that cost you, what
did you try before that, why did you stop. Past behaviour is evidence; stated intent is not.

**Ask what they've already paid for.** The strongest signal of demand is money someone has
already spent trying to solve this — including on solutions that failed them.

**Don't pitch during research.** The moment you pitch, they start being nice and the data
stops. Separate research conversations from sales conversations.

## Competitor Analysis

The purpose is not a comparison chart. It is to find where a competitor is structurally
unable to follow you.

**For each significant competitor, establish:** what they sell and at what price, who they
serve best and worst, what customers praise and complain about, how they acquire customers,
and what they are structurally committed to. That last one is the useful one — a competitor
with a large sales force cannot easily go self-serve, and a competitor serving enterprises
cannot easily serve small businesses profitably.

**Study successful companies in the category, not just local rivals.** If you want to open a
coffee shop, examine what the large chains actually optimise — location economics, throughput
per square foot, labour per transaction, average ticket. You will not beat them at their game,
but their game reveals which variables matter, and where a small operator can be better:
quality, specificity, community, service.

**Mystery-shop your competitors.** Buy from them. The experience tells you more than any
research: how fast they respond, what the handoff feels like, where it's annoying.

**Where to actually look:** their website and pricing page, their reviews, their job postings
(which reveal strategy and scale), their ads, their social presence, and their customers.

## SWOT — Using It Without Wasting Time

SWOT lists Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. Strengths and weaknesses are
internal and within your control; opportunities and threats are external and are not.

Most SWOTs are useless because they stop at listing. The value is in the pairing:
- Strength + Opportunity: where to attack, and where to concentrate resources.
- Weakness + Threat: what could actually kill you, and what to fix or hedge first.
- Strength + Threat: what defends you.
- Weakness + Opportunity: what to build or hire before you can move.

**Be specific or don't bother.** "Good customer service" is not a strength unless you can
show it is better than the alternatives in a way customers notice and pay for.

# SECTION 27: UNFAIR ADVANTAGE AND COMPETITIVE EDGE

## What an Unfair Advantage Actually Is

An unfair advantage — sometimes called an edge, a competitive advantage, or a moat — is
something you have that a competitor cannot easily buy or copy. The test is simple: if a
well-funded competitor decided tomorrow to take it from you, how long would it take and what
would it cost them? If the answer is "a few weeks and not much," it is not an advantage.

**Things that are usually not unfair advantages,** despite being claimed constantly: working
harder, caring more, better quality, good customer service, passion, a better website, being
first (unless you convert it into something durable), or a feature.

**Things that genuinely are:**

- **Proprietary access** — exclusive inventory, an exclusive territory, a licence, a supply
  relationship nobody else can get, data nobody else has.
- **An existing audience** — a list, a community, or a following you built. This is the most
  common real advantage for small operators and the most underrated.
- **Personal reputation and relationships** in a specific niche, where trust takes years.
- **Specific expertise** — a rare combination of skills, or deep domain knowledge from having
  worked inside the industry you now sell to.
- **Structural cost advantage** — you own the equipment, the building, or the process that
  lets you deliver cheaper.
- **Switching costs or network effects** built into the product.
- **Speed of learning** — a genuine advantage if the market is changing, and only if you
  actually run more experiments than competitors.

## Finding Yours

Ask, in order: What do I have access to that others don't? Who trusts me already? What do I
know from experience that a newcomer would take years to learn? What can I do faster or
cheaper because of something structural? What am I willing to do that competitors aren't —
a niche too small for them, a service too unglamorous, a customer too difficult?

**The last one is the most available to small businesses.** Larger competitors abandon
segments that are too small, too complex, or too low-margin for their cost structure. Being
the specialist in a niche a bigger player won't serve is a genuine and defensible advantage.

**If you don't have one yet, that is a finding, not a failure.** It means your plan must
include building one, and the plan should say which one and how.

# SECTION 28: VALIDATION AND FEASIBILITY

## Validate Before You Build

The purpose of validation is to spend the smallest amount of money and time that would
convince you to stop. Most people design tests that can only confirm what they hope.

**The order of evidence, weakest to strongest:** people say they like it → people say they
would buy → people give you their email → people join a waitlist with a deposit → people
pre-pay → people pay full price and use it repeatedly. Only the last two are conclusive.

**Sell it before you build it.** Take deposits, run a pre-sale, or sell the service manually
and deliver it by hand before automating anything. A signed cheque is the only reliable
market research.

**Set the kill criterion in advance.** Decide before the test what result would make you stop
— "if fewer than 5 of 50 prospects take a paid pilot, we don't proceed." Written down
beforehand, this is the single most effective defence against sunk-cost thinking.

## Running a Feasibility Study

A feasibility study asks whether this can work, before a business plan assumes it will. Four
questions, in order — a failure at any one stops the process:

**Market feasibility.** Is there enough demand, reachable at a cost you can afford? Evidence:
customer interviews, search volume, competitor count and health, pre-sales.

**Technical/operational feasibility.** Can you actually deliver it, at quality, at volume,
with the people and equipment available? Evidence: a pilot delivery, supplier quotes, a
staffing plan against real wage rates.

**Financial feasibility.** Do the unit economics work, and can you fund the gap until they
do? Evidence: bottom-up cost build, break-even volume, a monthly cash forecast through the
worst case.

**Legal and regulatory feasibility.** Licences, permits, zoning, insurance, and any
industry-specific regulation. Evidence: written confirmation from the relevant authority, not
a forum post.

**Write the conclusion as a decision, not a summary:** proceed, proceed with conditions, or
stop — and state what would change the answer.

## Startup Costs and the Cash Gap

**Count everything, then add a contingency.** Equipment, deposits, licences, insurance,
initial inventory, build-out, software, legal and accounting setup, branding, launch
marketing, and working capital. First-time estimates routinely miss working capital, which is
usually the largest item.

**The cash gap is the real number.** Not what it costs to open — what it costs to operate
from opening until revenue covers costs. Estimate how long that takes, then assume it takes
longer, because it usually does.

**Contingency of 20–30% on a build-out is conventional** and still frequently insufficient.
Running out of money during fit-out is a common and avoidable way to lose everything already
spent.

# SECTION 29: GO-TO-MARKET

## Sequencing the Launch

Go-to-market is the order in which you reach customers, not the list of every channel
available. Doing four channels badly beats nothing, but doing one well beats all of it.

**Start where trust already exists.** Your first customers should come from people who
already know you, people who know them, and communities you are already part of. This is
faster, cheaper, and produces the case studies that make colder channels work later.

**Then expand along the line of least resistance:** referrals from those first customers →
partnerships with people already serving your customer → one paid or content channel, tested
properly.

**Do not launch broadly before the offer converts.** Paid traffic to an offer that doesn't
convert buys you an expensive lesson. Prove the message works on a small scale first.

## Choosing the First Channel

**Match the channel to how the customer buys.** Urgent, searched-for problems (a burst pipe,
a legal deadline) are won on search and local listings. Considered purchases are won on
referral, reputation, and content. Impulse and discovery categories are won on social and
visual platforms. Selling to businesses with few, high-value customers is won by direct
outreach and relationships, not advertising.

**Estimate cost per customer before committing.** Roughly: what does it cost to get 100
prospects into the channel, what fraction become leads, what fraction become customers? If
the resulting cost per customer exceeds your gross profit per customer, the channel is wrong
regardless of how popular it is.

**Test cheaply, then concentrate.** Small tests across two or three plausible channels, then
put nearly everything behind whichever works. Spreading budget evenly across channels is how
businesses learn nothing about any of them.

## The First 90 Days

**Days 1–30:** sell manually to people you can reach directly. Talk to every customer
personally. The goal is evidence and learning, not volume.

**Days 31–60:** write down what worked — the message that landed, the objection that recurred,
the customer type that closed fastest. Turn it into a repeatable offer and a repeatable
pitch. Ask every satisfied customer for a referral and a testimonial.

**Days 61–90:** pick the one channel your evidence supports and invest in it properly. Put
the recurring operational work into a documented process.

**Track only what changes decisions:** leads by source, conversion rate at each step, cost per
customer, and cash. Everything else is decoration this early.
