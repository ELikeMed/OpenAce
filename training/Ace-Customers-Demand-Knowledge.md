# Ace Customers & Demand Knowledge Base
## Deciding Who to Sell To, and Where to Find Them

This volume covers defining an ideal customer, segmenting a market, judging whether demand
exists, sourcing leads, and building referral flow. It is industry-agnostic.

# SECTION 34: DEFINING THE IDEAL CUSTOMER

## Why "Everyone" Is the Most Expensive Answer

Selling to everyone means your message must be generic, which means it competes on price,
which means you win the least loyal customers. Narrowing the customer is what lets the message
get specific, and specificity is what makes marketing work at small budgets.

**The narrower the customer, the cheaper the marketing.** A message aimed at "small
businesses" has to outbid everyone for attention. A message aimed at "dental practices with
two or more locations that are hiring an associate" reaches fewer people, converts far more of
them, and can be delivered through channels that would be uneconomic for a broad audience.

**Narrowing is not shrinking.** You can still serve anyone who comes. You are choosing who to
*aim at*, which is a marketing decision, not a policy.

## Building an Ideal Customer Profile

An ideal customer profile describes the *company or household* worth targeting. For business
customers, describe: industry, size — revenue, headcount, or locations, geography, structure,
what makes them a fit, and what makes them a poor fit. For consumers: life stage, income band,
location, and the situation that creates the need.

**The most useful ICP is built from your own best existing customers, not imagined.** List
your ten most profitable, easiest, longest-retained customers. Then find what they share —
often it is something unexpected, like a stage of growth, a piece of software they run, a
recent event, or the role of the person who bought.

**Include the negative profile.** Who has been a bad customer — slow to pay, endlessly
demanding, quick to churn, always negotiating? Naming that pattern is as valuable as naming
the good one, because it tells you what to decline.

**The buyer is not always the user.** In business sales, identify who feels the pain, who
decides, who controls the budget, and who can block it. Marketing to the user while the
decision sits with someone else is a common and expensive misfire.

## Trigger Events — When Someone Becomes a Buyer

Most customers do not buy because they encountered your marketing. They buy because something
changed. Finding the trigger is often more valuable than finding the demographic.

**Common triggers:** rapid growth or a new location, new leadership or a new hire in a relevant
role, funding raised, a regulation or deadline, a failure or incident, a competitor's move, a
contract renewal date, seasonality, or a life event for consumer purchases.

**Practical use:** if you can identify the trigger, you can find people at the moment they are
receptive — through job postings, permit filings, licence records, funding announcements, news,
review complaints about a competitor, or simple timing. This is far more efficient than
broadcasting to people who might need you eventually.

## Segmentation

Segmenting means splitting a market into groups that behave differently enough to warrant
different offers, messages, or prices.

**Segment on behaviour and need, not just description.** Age and industry are easy to observe
but often don't predict buying. "Businesses that have been burned by a previous vendor" is a
better segment than "businesses in Florida," because it predicts what they need to hear.

**Practical segments to test:** by urgency, by sophistication, by size, by how they buy
(referral vs. search vs. relationship), and by which problem they lead with.

**Serve one segment properly before adding a second.** Each segment needs its own message and
often its own channel; running several at once with one budget is how none of them work.

# SECTION 35: JUDGING WHETHER DEMAND EXISTS

## Existing Demand vs. Created Demand

**Existing demand** means people are already searching for a solution and buying one. Your job
is to be found and to be the obvious choice. Marketing is comparatively cheap and fast because
the customer is already in motion.

**Created demand** means people have the problem but aren't looking for a solution — often
because they don't know one exists, or don't frame their situation as solvable. Marketing here
is education, it takes far longer, and it costs far more. Many businesses fail because they
budgeted for the first situation and were actually in the second.

**How to tell which you're in:** are people searching for it? Are there competitors making
money? Is there a category name customers themselves use? If yes to all three, demand exists
and the challenge is differentiation. If no, you are creating a category, which is a much
harder, slower, better-funded game.

**Competitors are evidence, not a warning.** A market with several healthy competitors is a
market with proven demand. A market with none usually means no demand rather than an untapped
opportunity — verify which before celebrating.

## Signals Worth Trusting

**Strongest to weakest:** people already paying for an inferior solution → people paying for a
manual workaround → active search volume → complaints on review sites and forums → stated
interest in a survey → your own conviction.

**Look for the workaround.** When people build a spreadsheet, hire someone part-time, or invent
a manual process to handle something, that is proven demand with a proven budget already
allocated.

**Read one- and two-star reviews of competitors.** This is the cheapest, most honest demand
research available: real customers describing exactly what is missing, in their own words.

# SECTION 36: FINDING LEADS

## Where Leads Actually Come From

For most small businesses, in rough order of quality: existing customers buying again,
referrals from customers, referrals from partners and other professionals, personal network,
inbound from search and reputation, community and events, targeted outreach, and finally paid
advertising. Most owners start at the bottom of this list and skip the top of it.

**Repeat and referral business is cheaper than everything else and most owners underwork it.**
Before spending on advertising, ask whether every past customer knows what else you offer, and
whether every satisfied customer has been asked for a referral.

## Building a Prospect List

**Define the ICP first, then source against it.** A list built before the profile is just names.

**Sources that work for business customers:** industry directories and association membership
lists, licence and permit registries, review platforms, professional networks, local chambers,
trade show exhibitor lists, job boards — which reveal both growth and pain — and map-based
local search for geographic businesses.

**Verify before you contact.** Confirm the business still operates, the contact still holds the
role, and the details are current. Outreach to stale data damages sender reputation and wastes
the effort that follows.

**Quality beats volume decisively.** Fifty well-researched prospects with a specific, relevant
reason for contact outperform two thousand generic ones, and cost less to work.

**Never fabricate a lead.** An invented company name or contact is worse than an empty list: it
wastes time, corrupts the pipeline, and destroys trust in every other record. A short honest
list is the correct outcome when the market is small.

## Outreach That Works

**Lead with something specific to them.** A first line that could be sent to anyone will be
treated as if it was. Reference something real — their recent expansion, a review theme, a job
posting, a change in their industry.

**Offer a next step proportionate to the relationship.** A stranger will not book an hour. Ask
for something small: a question answered, a short call, a useful resource.

**Follow up more than feels comfortable, then stop cleanly.** Most replies come after the first
contact, and most people give up before that. A defined sequence — several touches over a few
weeks, then a polite close — respects both their time and yours.

**Channel follows customer.** Trades and local services respond to phone. Professional services
respond to email and referral. Younger consumer markets respond to social and messaging. Use
the channel the customer already uses, not the one you prefer.

## Referral Systems

Referrals are the highest-converting, lowest-cost source of customers and are almost always
left to chance.

**Ask at the moment of satisfaction** — right after a good result, not at some quarterly
interval. That is when willingness peaks.

**Ask specifically.** "Do you know anyone who might need this?" produces nothing. "You
mentioned your colleague at the other practice is dealing with the same scheduling problem —
would you be comfortable introducing me?" produces an introduction.

**Make it effortless.** Provide the exact wording they can forward. People want to help and
don't want to compose an email.

**Build partner referrals with people who serve your customer but don't compete.** Accountants
and attorneys, plumbers and electricians, realtors and mortgage brokers, designers and
developers. Give first and give consistently — a partner who receives referrals from you sends
them back, and the relationship compounds far beyond any advertising channel.

**Thank people visibly and promptly.** Referral flow stops when it goes unacknowledged, and
restarting it is much harder than maintaining it.
